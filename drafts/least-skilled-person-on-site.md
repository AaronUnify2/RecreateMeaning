# The Least Skilled Person on Site

It's late afternoon and the tradesmen have gone home. I can swing a hammer and I know roughly what everyone out here is doing. What I don't know is the hundred small judgment calls a roofer makes before lunch. But I do know what a qualified sparky costs per hour, and I'm not paying that rate for a man to carry offcuts to a skip bin.

So I work after they've gone, because a homeowner on site is its own kind of obstruction. Nobody works loose with the client watching. Stand there long enough and you get performance instead of work — people optimising for looking busy, because looking busy is what you can see. Worse, they stop telling you bad news. Nobody volunteers "I stuffed that up" to the man holding the chequebook. My presence costs me money in ways that never appear on an invoice, so I schedule myself around it.

## The map is missing

The standard advice for a system that isn't flowing is to find the constraint and fix it. Excellent advice, if you can see the constraint. Standing in a half-built house, I could not have told you what it was. I couldn't tell you what the concrete was up to. I couldn't tell you whether the roof was ahead or behind. The thing that limits this build might be a delivery date, a council inspection, or the fact that two trades need the same space on Thursday.

This is the situation most people are actually in, most of the time. Not "here is the bottleneck, go fix it," but "here is a large complicated thing I don't understand, and I'd like it to go better."

The usual response is to think harder. Stand back, gather information, build a picture, and act once the picture is complete. This feels responsible. It is mostly a way of running a bad simulation of something that is sitting right there, willing to answer questions for free. The site will tell you things. It just won't tell you until you touch it.

The catch is that touching things is how you break them. So the search isn't for the bottleneck. The search is for the cheapest honest experiment — the move that teaches you something and costs almost nothing if you've got it wrong. Stacking scrap timber is one. Rewiring the switchboard yourself is very much not.

## The two things you can see

Here's the part nobody tells you. You arrive, you scan the site, and you can see maybe two things you're qualified to do. That feels like a limitation. It isn't. You do the two things, and in doing them you find the edge of what you understand, which is the only place understanding ever grows from. Stack the timber and you learn what's scrap and what someone was saving. And when you look up, there are four more jobs you couldn't see an hour ago.

Some of that is literal. Clearing a site increases visibility, in the plain sense that you can now see the floor. Jobs that were always there become findable once they're not buried. But the more interesting part is that sorting forces discrimination. You cannot stack a pile of timber without deciding, piece by piece, what counts as rubbish. Make that decision forty times and you have learned the categories. You didn't study them. You acquired them by handling the material.

Do it once and it's a chore. Do it twenty times and it's an education. Each pass raises the floor of what you can see, which means the next pass finds better work than the last. Nobody decides this should happen. It just compounds.

## Why the owner should be the one holding the broom

I'm the least skilled person on this site. I'm also the only one whose interest is the whole thing. The roofer is optimising the roof, the sparky the wiring, and they're right to — that's what I'm paying for. But nobody's job is the site. Nobody but me is going to look at a pile of old trusses and see timber I might want in five years, or notice that tomorrow's trailer has nowhere to park. That's not a skill. It's a position. And it's the one job on this build that can't be subcontracted.

The trusses are a good example. We've gone to steel for the roof, so the old timber is waste — correctly so, to a roofer, whose job ends at the roof. To me it's a maybe, because I'm the one who'll be standing here in five years wanting a shelf, and it's already cut to the dimensions of this house. Stacked in a pile off to the side, it stops being clutter in everyone's way and becomes something any trade on site can pull from. The carpenter needs a bit of packing timber, it's there. Nothing about the timber changed. I didn't add value. I added order, and the resource was always in the pile, just unreadable.

And the hours I spend doing this buy back hours I was going to pay for anyway. Every hour a roofer spends tidying is an hour of roofer rates spent on labourer work, and it draws down a finite supply of his good attention. You can't buy more of a tradesman's day. You can change what fraction of it is skilled.

## The invoice that never arrives

None of this is really about houses. Most of us spend our working lives inside a system with a defined role in it, measured against that role, and quietly discouraged from looking sideways.

Organisations pour enormous attention into job roles. People are expected to be masters of their own domain, and mostly they are. A shockingly small amount of attention goes into how the domains fit together. So repairs get made at the point of failure — everyone patches the seam in front of them, nobody looks at the seams — and the disorder accumulates as a kind of debt.

Disorder never sends you an invoice. It shows up as everything taking slightly longer than it should, forever, and nobody ever files a complaint about slightly. Every role passes its own audit. Every person is doing their job. And the whole thing crawls, at a cost no one can point to, until someone happens to stand in exactly the right spot and see it. Which can take years, and often takes never.

Which is the argument for doing something when you spot it. If it's a small improvement, fine, you've made a small improvement. But there's a real chance you've stumbled onto something major, and the fact that it revealed itself to *you* is information. Bottlenecks reveal themselves to whoever is standing at them. If you can see it, you're probably the person paying for it, which means you have both the motive and the vantage. Waiting for the right person to notice usually means waiting for nobody.

The selfish version of the argument is better anyway: you'll get better at the job they actually measure you on. You can't see how your slice connects to the rest until you've walked around a bit.

## The immune system

Systems resist change, and they're usually right to. Most proposed changes are bad. The scepticism you run into is, more often than not, doing its job.

Sometimes the defence misfires and attacks the repairs too. That can be self-interest — a change that improves the whole and shrinks somebody's role will find an articulate enemy. But it's just as often something duller. I managed a restaurant for a while and had people bring me good ideas at times when I had to say: that's a good idea, I'm halfway through changing something else, and I need to see it through before I touch anything new. If I change three things at once and the numbers move, I've learned nothing. I've spent my feedback and bought noise.

Nobody enjoys hearing that. They want me to have more bandwidth than I have. But the restraint is the same principle in reverse — honest feedback is the scarce resource, and you protect it by going one at a time. At scale you don't serialise, you isolate; that's all an A/B test is, a way to change many things at once without losing the signal. One variable per observer.

## Chaos you can't read

One caution, and it's the one I'd want flagged if it were my site. A system you can't read is not the same as a system that isn't working. I've watched people walk into an operation, see what looks like pure chaos, and start improving it — when the chaos was theirs and the place was shipping fine. If it's producing output and it looks like madness to you, the madness is in your model. You don't shut down the production line because you don't know how everything works.

Usually the gap between incomprehensible and readable is a handful of rules, not a thousand facts. Chess has six pieces. Nobody who's had the moves explained finds a game hard to follow, and it's the same board that looked like noise ten minutes earlier.

Which is why nobody reads the manual for a video game. That's not laziness, it's correct — failure is free, so mashing buttons is the fastest way to learn the rules. Real systems charge you for failure, which is the whole reason you start with the timber. Same learning loop, different price, and the price is what you're managing.

## Go and touch it

Two honest caveats. This works best where feedback is quick and clean. The timber moved or it didn't. In slow or political systems the feedback arrives late, noisy, and sometimes shaped by people with an interest in what you conclude, and acting to learn can teach you the wrong lesson with great confidence. And it's easier for me than for most, because it's my house. Standing helps. A labourer doing what I did this afternoon would need permission he probably wouldn't get.

But inside those limits, I'd back this over thinking about it. And I'd add that people are not usually held back by a shortage of competence. They're held back by overestimating the cost of being wrong in public. Which is a strange thing to fear, given how cheap most first moves are, and how reversible.

For what it's worth, I started this build by picking up rubbish. I've since given myself an apprenticeship off the back of it, working up from filling cracks to resheeting whole rooms. Whether I'm any good at it is not yet established. I'll find out over the next few years, as the cracks either appear or don't.

That's the deal, and it's a better deal than it sounds. You're not risking being wrong. You're buying information, and wrong is the cheaper way to get it. Reality's rejection is still a reply.
