# Widening the Neck

Tip a bottle upside down. Everything inside wants out, and the width of the neck decides how fast that happens. You can make the bottle bigger. You can shake it, plead with it, buy a nicer bottle. Nothing moves faster until the neck gets wider.

Most systems have a neck like this — one point that sets the pace for everything upstream of it. The formal name is the limiting constraint. I call it the bottleneck, because I can picture a bottle and I can't picture a limiting constraint. The good news is that necks can be widened. The bad news is that you have to find the right one first.

It's worth being clear about the size of the prize here, because I don't want to undersell it. Shipping containers are a box with standardised corners. That's the whole innovation. It widened one neck in global logistics and went on to reshape trade, retail, manufacturing and the geography of half the world's cities. One boring standardisation. No new physics.

That's the upper bound, and most necks are not that. But the pattern holds at every scale, and the second thing worth being clear about is that nobody is going to hand you the brief. There's no permission step. You don't need to be told how to interact with a system, and you don't need anyone to confirm your diagnosis before you're allowed to look. You can learn to spot these yourself. Here's how I do it.

## Finding it

The method is the one I've written about elsewhere, so I'll be quick. Sort the field into what you can touch and what you can't, and start at the cheap end. Do the easy, reversible, low-consequence jobs — the ones where being wrong costs you an afternoon. Each one teaches you something about how the thing actually runs, and each one makes the next layer visible.

What you're waiting for is repetition. Keep contact up long enough and the same obstruction starts showing up in unrelated places. The scheduler complains about it, and so does the bloke on the floor, and so does the invoice at the end of the month, and none of them are talking to each other.

That's the diagnostic, and it's the most useful line in this article: symptoms scatter, the constraint doesn't. A dozen unrelated complaints with one thing in common is not a coincidence. It's triangulation. You're not wandering hopefully around a system — you're running a search with a target, and the small jobs are your instrumentation.

## The size of the neck tells you nothing about the size of the fix

There's a habit worth breaking: sizing the solution by the size of the problem.

A constraint can be strangling an entire operation and still come loose with an afternoon's work and forty dollars of hardware. It happens constantly, and people refuse to believe it — surely something this expensive deserves a serious response. So they convene, and plan, and cost a rebuild, while the tape that would have restored flow sits in a drawer.

The same error runs the other way. A small, embarrassing little neck sometimes needs a genuinely expensive fix, and nobody will fund it, because the problem doesn't look like it's worth that much.

Either way the mistake is the same. You size a solution by the mechanism, not by the damage.

And there's a reason the big fix keeps winning that isn't about merit at all. Suppose a company announces a new facility to add capacity. That sounds like growth. Now suppose it announces that it was one mop bucket short, and consequently produced two hundred thousand dollars less output per day for a year. Same money recovered. One is a triumph and the other is a confession, and it's a lot easier to buy a mop bucket than to build a facility.

The politics compound it. A rebuild gives fifteen people a role and a chance to shine, each responsible for their own piece of the new thing. Fixing two pages gives one person an afternoon and no credit. Worse, once the budget for the big solution is approved, quietly improving the current version starts to look like an argument against the budget — so the broken thing gets left broken on purpose, by people who all believe they're behaving reasonably. That's the immune system again, this time wearing a business case.

## Multiply before you redesign

When you do have the neck located, the reflex is to redesign the station. Usually you can just build a second one.

Duplicating is underrated because it's inelegant. It feels like admitting the original wasn't designed properly, where a redesign feels like engineering. But the duplicate is reversible, cheap, and built on something you already know works. The redesign is a bet, and you're placing it with the understanding you had *before* you fixed anything.

Nor should you expect the line to be balanced in the first place. If your process has three steps, there's no reason step two should have the same capacity as steps one and three. It takes longer because of what it is, not because someone did it wrong. Variability between steps is the normal condition of any production line, and almost all productive work becomes a production line as it gets more organised. So duplicating the slow station is the precise move. You're not scaling the line. You're widening the neck and leaving the rest alone.

Then something interesting happens. One eye gives you vision; two give you depth. Two stations running the same job don't just double throughput — they give you comparison. Same work, two setups, and now you can see which is better, which was invisible when there was only one. You built the second station for capacity and got a free experiment as a byproduct.

That's the theme of this whole approach, if it has one. Cheap moves keep paying you in information you didn't plan for.

## Precision is the lever

Which brings me to the test I'd apply before committing to anything expensive.

If the fix you're contemplating is enormous, that's evidence you haven't found the neck yet. Big solutions are often just imprecise ones — you're widening the whole bottle because you can't see where it narrows. The better your diagnosis, the smaller the intervention you can get away with, and the cheaper it is to be wrong about.

So it's worth going back and resolving the picture a little further before you spend. All that unglamorous contact — the tidying, the small jobs, the things you did because they were the only things available — was never really about the jobs. It was about getting the image sharp enough that the neck had an address.

Find the address and the fix is usually small. Sometimes it's a mop bucket.
